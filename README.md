## Abstract

Does a change in housing tenure accompany a change in how serious a respondent judges the national housing problem, and is the association attributable to tenure rather than to household formation, migration or early-adult life-course transitions? We use the 2014--2020 China Family Panel Studies: 98,166 person-wave observations from 32,417 adults, person fixed effects and baseline-PSU clustered inference. The pre-specified rent test yields a bounded null: 10\% higher monthly rent is associated with at most 0.03 points on the 0--10 severity scale, and equivalence holds within 0.05 within-person standard deviations. The pre-specified joint test on the eight tenure categories and additional property rejects but carries little information. A post hoc aligned comparison shows a within-person renter--owner difference of 0.348 points, or 0.14 within-person standard deviations, against 0.984 points in pooled regressions. The contrast is not an artefact of household splits, migration or early-adult life-course transitions: across four restrictions that remove them, and repeated switching, it is unchanged or larger, between 0.332 and 0.391 points. Renters are less likely to be observed twice (70.9\% versus 82.4\% retention), but inverse-probability weighting moves the estimate only from 0.297 to 0.299. These are conditional within-person associations, not causal effects of ownership, and they describe a retained panel rather than the Chinese population. Four US surveys serve as an external reference only: the same respondents answer questions about desired prices and about specific developments differently, so the severity rating is not read as a proxy for policy positions.
